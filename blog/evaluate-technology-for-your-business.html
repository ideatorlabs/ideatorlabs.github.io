How to tell if a new tool will pay off in your business

A simple way to judge a tool against how things work today.

In this article

  3. Will it work with what you already use?

A new tool pays off when it fixes one problem you can measure today, works with the systems and data you already have, and costs less in total than the time or money it saves. You can check all three before you sign: write down how the work is done now, run a short trial on real work with the people who will use it, and compare the result with your own starting number.

Key takeaways

  • Start with one problem and one number from today (hours a week, errors a month, money lost), not with a product demo.
  • Count the full cost: subscription, setup, moving your data in, training, support and your team’s time.
  • Trial on real work, ask how your data is protected and how you get it back, and only then decide.

What should you write down before you look at any tool?

Write down the one problem you want fixed and how it is handled today: who does it, how many hours a week it takes, and what goes wrong. “Two people spend Monday mornings re-typing orders into the accounts” is a problem you can measure. “We need to digitise” is not.

Then split what you need into must-haves and nice-to-haves, the way the Canadian Federation of Independent Business suggests in its checklist for owners (CFIB, 10 October 2025). A tool that misses a must-have is out, however good the demo looks.

How do you work out what a tool will really cost?

Add up everything, not just the monthly price. CFIB lists the subscription or purchase, any new hardware, implementation or training fees, and ongoing support and maintenance (CFIB). Add your own team’s time to learn and run the tool, and the cost of moving your existing data in.

Then put a rupee value on what it saves, using the number you wrote down at the start.

Example (made-up figures to show the sum, not a benchmark): a tool costs ₹2,000 a month plus ₹10,000 to set up. It saves one person 6 hours a week, and that person’s time costs the business ₹300 an hour. That is about ₹7,800 a month saved (6 hours × 4.33 weeks × ₹300), or ₹5,800 after the subscription, so the setup cost is earned back in about two months. If the saving were 1 hour a week, it would never pay back.

Will it work with what you already use?

Check that the tool passes data to and from the systems you already run, such as your accounts, billing or sales records. A US Small Business Administration checklist puts it plainly: the last thing you want is to re-enter data from one system into another by hand (SBA blog, 2018).

Ask three things: can your existing data be imported, will the vendor help with the import (CFIB), and can you export all of it again, in a usable format, if you leave. A tool you can’t leave is a cost you can’t control.

How do you run a fair trial?

  1. Use real work. Last week’s orders, this month’s invoices, real customer records (with permission), not the vendor’s sample data.
  2. Involve the people who will use it. CFIB advises bringing in the staff who will use the system and narrowing the choice to three or four suppliers (CFIB).
  3. Cover one full cycle. A week or two for daily work; the SBA checklist suggests free trials of about that length (SBA blog). For month-end work, run it through a month-end.
  4. Measure the same number as before. Hours, errors or money, counted the same way.
  5. Prefer low-risk terms. Month-to-month payment or the right to cancel means a bad fit doesn’t become a long bill (SBA blog). Get trial length and refund terms in writing (CFIB).

What should you ask the vendor about your data?

If the tool handles your customers’ or staff’s personal data, you stay responsible for it. India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 says a business may use a data processor for that data only under a valid contract, and must protect personal data, including what a processor handles on its behalf, with reasonable security safeguards (sections 8(2) and 8(5), DPDP Act, 2023, Gazette text on MeitY). This is not legal advice.

The UK National Cyber Security Centre publishes a plain list of questions to ask suppliers (NCSC: supplier assurance questions). For a small business, these are the ones that matter most:

  • Who at the vendor is responsible for security, and who can see your data?
  • Have they had a security breach they should tell you about?
  • How do they back up and recover if they are attacked, and how quickly will they tell you about an incident?
  • Do their staff have only the access they need to do their job?

When is the answer “not yet”?

Wait if you can’t name the problem or the number it should move. Wait if the work itself is muddled, because a tool makes a muddled process faster, not better. And walk away if the vendor won’t say how your data is protected or how you get it back, or if the full cost is more than the saving you can show.

Sometimes the right answer is a better spreadsheet and a clear owner for the task. That is a fine result: it costs little, and the number you wrote down tells you when it is time to look again.

Questions owners ask

How long should a software trial run before I decide?

Long enough to cover one full cycle of the work: a week or two for daily tasks, a full month-end for accounting work. Run it on real work, with the people who will use it, and compare against the number you wrote down before the trial.

What costs do owners usually miss when buying software?

Setup, moving existing data in, training, any new hardware, ongoing support, and the staff time spent learning and running the tool. Add these to the subscription before you compare it with what the tool saves.

What should I ask a vendor about my customers’ data?

Where the data is stored, who at the vendor can see it, how it is protected and backed up, what happens if they have a breach, and how you get all your data out if you leave. Put the answers in the contract.
