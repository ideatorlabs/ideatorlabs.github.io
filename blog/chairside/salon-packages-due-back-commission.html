Why salons lose regular clients and money on packages and commission, and how to stop it

Three leaks in one salon: clients who drift away, package uses nobody can prove, and commission argued at month-end.

In this article
  1. Why do salons lose regular clients?
  2. How often should a client come back?

  4. What happens when a client’s balance is wrong?
  5. Why do commission disputes keep coming back?
  6. What should an owner put in place?
  7. How does Chairside do this?
  8. Questions owners ask

Salons lose money on prepaid packages when no one can prove that a use really happened, and they lose regulars when no one owns the list of who is due back. Commission disputes start when stylists are paid from a month-end sheet nobody checked. The fixes are plain: get the client to confirm each package use, keep a due-back list the desk works every day, and pay commission only from checked lines under rules written in advance.

Key takeaways

  • A package use counts only when the client confirms it on their own phone. Count manager confirmations separately.
  • Give every service a return interval and work a due-back list daily, so regulars do not depend on a stylist’s memory.
  • Write the commission rule down (whole month or excess, menu price or amount paid) and pay only from checked lines.

Why do salons lose regular clients?

Most are not lost to a bad service. They drift, because nobody is tracking who should have been back by now. We found no published return rate for an Indian salon chain, so here is what outside data shows.

Boulevard, a salon software vendor, looked at January 2022 to March 2023 data from its own platform. About 45% of first-time clients at the average salon booked a second appointment, against 70% at the top tenth. Clients whose first visit was booked online came back 78% of the time, walk-ins 39% (Salon Today report on Boulevard data; vendor data, region not stated). Phorest, another vendor, gives about 30% retention for new clients and 68% for existing ones, calling them approximate benchmarks from its own research (Phorest, January 2023).

These are not Indian numbers and come from vendors, so treat them as direction, not a target.

How often should a client come back?

It depends on the service, so set an interval for each one. Keune suggests refreshing colour roots every 4 to 6 weeks, and every 4 weeks for strong contrast such as dark dye over grey (Keune). Eminence Organics suggests a facial every four to six weeks, since skin renews in about 28 days (Eminence Organics). Both are Western brand content; we found no Indian interval study.

Whatever your intervals are, message the client a few days before the date, and record what happened: booked, not now, or lost with a reason.

How do prepaid packages leak money?

A package is cash you have taken and a service you still owe. The leak is a use that did not happen. MioSalon, a salon software vendor, describes staff redeeming a dormant client’s balance against a paying walk-in and keeping the cash, or selling a large package cheaply to a friend and redeeming it against regular clients. Its red flags are packages used fast, uses clustered on one or two staff, and a very low sale price for the value (MioSalon blog).

The usual defence is an OTP to the client’s phone. Lakmé Salon’s terms say redemption needs an OTP sent to the registered mobile, and that without it the client pays in full (Lakmé Salon membership terms). In software the OTP is a setting, and MioSalon itself warns it fails if the OTP goes to a number the manager controls.

What happens when a client’s balance is wrong?

They complain, and sometimes they win. A Chandigarh consumer commission ordered a health club and spa to refund Rs 44,250 with 9% interest after it changed a membership’s validity without telling the member (Tribune, 28 September 2022). Hong Kong’s Consumer Council received 77 complaints in a year about prepaid hair-salon services and told customers to keep their own log of sessions used (Consumer Council, April 2019).

Why do commission disputes keep coming back?

Because the rule is vague or the working is hidden. A payroll guide for Indian salons says doing tiered commission by hand for 8 to 15 staff is where most owners make errors, with staff disputes and loss of trust as the result (SalaryBox payroll guide).

Two gaps cause most arguments, according to a Petpooja template. A slab contract often does not say whether crossing the line pays on everything that month or only on the excess. And commission on menu price after a discount means the salon funds the discount twice (Petpooja commission template).

Example (a sum for illustration, using the SalaryBox guide’s numbers): a stylist with a Rs 20,000 base has a target of Rs 60,000 (3 times base) and does Rs 85,000 of service. At 15% on the excess, the commission is 15% of Rs 25,000, which is Rs 3,750. If the slab paid on the whole month, it would be 15% of Rs 85,000, which is Rs 12,750. Same month, a Rs 9,000 gap. This is why the contract has to say which one.

What should an owner put in place?

  1. Confirm every package use with the client. Use their own phone, and track how many uses a manager confirmed instead.
  2. Watch the patterns. Cheap packages, fast use, and one stylist doing most package work are worth a look, as questions and not accusations.
  3. Keep a due-back list per outlet. One return interval per service, one named person at the desk, a reason logged for every client lost.
  4. Pay commission only from lines someone checked, such as the POS sales file, and let each stylist see their lines and working.
  5. Write the rules. Whole month or excess, menu price or amount paid, who settles a dispute and in how many hours.
  6. Read one summary per outlet each morning instead of eight WhatsApp groups.

How does Chairside do this?

Chairside works next to your POS. For a package use, the desk starts the use and the client scans a QR code or opens a WhatsApp link on their own phone and taps yes or no. A use with no answer in 12 hours, a refusal, or one phone confirming for several clients becomes a check for the outlet manager. A manager cannot confirm a use they made themselves.

Each checked visit sets a due-back date from the service’s return interval. Three days before, the desk gets a WhatsApp message ready to send from its own phone, and the item closes only when a checked visit is recorded. Commission is worked from checked lines only, with percentage, fixed or monthly-target rules, and the choice of excess or whole month. A stylist can dispute a line, the manager or owner settles it within 72 hours, and the approved statement is frozen. At 8 am the owner gets a digest per outlet.

Two limits: the confirmation is not yet tied to the client’s number (that needs a paid WhatsApp Business API provider), and the POS connection is a daily sales file, not live.

Questions owners ask

How do I stop staff using a client’s package when the client was not there?

Ask the client to confirm each use on their own phone, and keep the confirmations apart from uses a manager confirmed. Watch for packages sold very cheaply, packages used fast, and uses clustered on one or two staff. An OTP only helps if it reaches the client’s real number.

How often should a salon bring regular clients back?

It depends on the service. Brands and skin-care educators suggest about every 4 to 6 weeks for colour root touch-ups and for facials. Set a return interval per service and message the client a few days before it ends. We found no Indian study of these intervals.

How do I avoid commission disputes with stylists?

Write the rule down, including whether a target slab pays on the whole month or only on the excess, and whether commission is on the menu price or on what the client paid. Pay only from lines that were checked, and give each stylist a view of their own lines.
