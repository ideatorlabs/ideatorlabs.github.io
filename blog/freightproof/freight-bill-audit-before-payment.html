How to audit freight bills before you pay a transporter

Five places bills go wrong, how to check each one, and how to get overpayments back.

Transporter bills go wrong in five ordinary ways: the same LR billed twice, a rate that is not the one in force, extra charges with no proof, a fuel surcharge worked out on the wrong base, and weight that is not what was loaded. The fix is to check every line against the LR record and the rate card before payment, hold only the amount in doubt, and track each overcharge as a claim until a credit note or set-off lands in the books.

In short

  • Check each bill line against its LR and the rate card version in force on the LR date, then against past bills for the same LR.
  • Raise a claim before you pay. Public audit data shows overcharges raised late are collected less often.
  • A claim needs an owner, a deadline, evidence and a recorded credit note or set-off, or it will stall.

Where do freight bills go wrong?

Most Indian transporters are small. Around 75% of truck operators own fewer than five trucks (Redseer), so rate cards sit in emails, LR copies arrive by hand and bills are often built from memory.

No public Indian study measures how much of a shipper’s freight bill is wrong. A freight-audit software vendor, RoaDo, publishes its own estimates as a share of invoice value: rate misapplied 2–4%, extra charges 1–3%, fuel surcharge 0.5–2%, weight 2–5% (RoaDo guide). These are vendor claims, not audited findings. Measure your own bills before you trust any of them.

How do duplicate LRs get paid twice?

A duplicate is usually not a copy of the same bill. It is the same LR on a later bill, a re-issued bill after a rate change, or a supplementary bill that repeats a trip. A US materials-handling article puts double billing at up to 2% of invoices (Modern Materials Handling); it gives no Indian figure.

The check is simple and tiring by hand: every LR number on a new bill must be searched in earlier bills from that transporter, not only the last one. Keep a ledger of bill lines by LR, and look back a year or more.

How does a wrong rate get through?

A contract is revised and the transporter bills at the old rate, or the wrong vehicle size or slab is used. The test is the rate card version in force on the LR date, not on the bill date or today.

Example sum (made-up numbers): the rate card says ₹29,500 for a lane and truck size, and the bill line says ₹32,000. The claim is ₹2,500 on that LR. Ten such lines in a month is ₹25,000, small each time and large over a year.

Is a detention charge fair?

Only if the contract allows it and the hours are shown. One trucking booking platform says the free window is typically 2 to 4 hours at each point, with ₹200–500 an hour after that depending on truck size, and that detention should appear as a separate line with the hours documented (TruckGuru). There is no national standard, so your own contract decides.

Ask for the gate-in and gate-out times or the dated POD. A detention line with no times is a question, not a payable amount.

How do you check a fuel surcharge?

First find out which method your contract uses. One carrier’s published rate card sets fuel surcharge at 10% of base freight and says it is not fixed and may change with diesel prices (Eshopbox Cargo). Truckload contracts often index freight to diesel by a factor; a vendor blog quotes a transporters’ association leader saying most customers still use factors of 0.4 to 0.5, while the association proposed 0.65 (FreightFox).

If your contract does not state the base price, the factor and the date the price is read, the surcharge cannot be audited. Write those three things into the rate card.

How are weight disputes settled?

Part-load bills charge on the higher of actual and volumetric weight. The same Eshopbox card sets a minimum chargeable weight of 20 kg and 1 CFT as 6 kg, with CFT worked out as length × breadth × height in cm divided by 27,000, and labour union charges “chargeable as actuals” (Eshopbox Cargo). Other carriers use other rules, so use the ones in your own contract.

Compare the billed weight with the weight on the LR and your dispatch record. If the LR shows less than the bill, that is the claim.

How do you audit before paying, and recover what was overpaid?

  1. Match every line to its LR record, the rate card version in force on that date and earlier bills.
  2. Open a claim for each unsupported amount with the bill line, the contract row, the working and the source files kept as they were.
  3. Give each claim an owner and a date for deciding, sending and settling, with a named person above them when it slips.
  4. Send one pack per transporter with every claim, its evidence and a reply-by date, written plainly. Small transporters are partners, not targets.
  5. Hold the claimed amount from the next payment run, and pay the undisputed rest.
  6. Close on the books. Record the credit note or set-off, and have a second person check it against Tally or the transporter ledger.

Speed matters. A US General Services Administration audit of federal freight found under 10% of overcharges uncollected when raised within 400 days of payment, over 25% at 600 to 799 days, and nearly 35% at 800 days or more (GSA Inspector General, 2014). That is US federal data, not Indian.

Two rules shape how you recover. Only the transporter can issue a GST credit note, and it must be declared by 30 November after the financial year of the supply (CGST Act, section 34). And for micro and small transporters, payment is due by the agreed date, at most 45 days from acceptance, with compound interest at three times the bank rate if late; acceptance is deemed after 15 days unless you object in writing (MSMED Act 2006, sections 2, 15 and 16). Raise claims fast, in writing, and have your CA confirm the treatment.

How does FreightProof do this?

FreightProof checks bills against LR records and rate cards, including the rate card version in force on the LR date and the past 400 days of that transporter’s bills for repeats. Each unsupported amount becomes a claim with an owner, a deadline and frozen evidence, and open claims are held back on the pay list. A claim closes only when a credit note or set-off is recorded and a second person checks it. A bill line with no LR record gets no weight or detention claim, and reading gate times from dispatch is planned, not built.

Questions finance and logistics heads ask

Should I hold a whole transporter bill if one line is wrong?

Hold only the disputed amount where you can, and pay the rest on time. For a micro or small transporter, the MSMED Act sets a payment limit of 45 days from acceptance, so send a written objection within 15 days of delivery. Ask your CA how a part-disputed bill is treated.

Can a shipper issue a GST credit note for an overcharge?

No. Under section 34 of the CGST Act the supplier, here the transporter, issues the credit note, and must declare it by 30 November after the financial year of the supply. A commercial settlement or set-off against a later bill is the other route; confirm the treatment with your CA.
