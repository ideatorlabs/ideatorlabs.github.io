How credit teams spot edited bank statements before sanction

The checks that catch an edited statement, and the notes and sign-off that prove you ran them.

In this article

Credit teams spot an edited bank statement by checking three things in order: whether every balance adds up row by row, whether the file’s own facts (the program that wrote it, its dates, how often it was saved) fit a statement downloaded from a bank, and whether the money flow fits the business. Each finding needs a named person’s note and a second person’s sign-off. That note trail is what an auditor will ask for later.

In short

  • Run the arithmetic first: opening balance plus credits minus debits must equal the closing balance, on every row.
  • Read the file as well as the figures: producing program, creation and modification dates, and whether two statements of one bank were written by different programs.
  • Record every flag as explained or confirmed with a note, have a different person sign off, and keep the file’s hash so you can show which statement you reviewed.

Which checks catch an edited statement?

Three layers catch most edits, and each is a check you can repeat on every file. None needs a judgement about the borrower.

  1. Balance chain. Opening balance plus credits minus debits should equal the closing balance, and each row’s running balance should follow from the one above. An edited amount breaks the chain at that row and every row after it. Precisa, a statement-analysis vendor, describes the same check.
  2. File facts. Look at which program wrote the PDF, when it was created and last modified, and how many times it was saved. A statement from a bank’s system that shows an editor or word processor as its producer, or a modification date days after creation and close to the application date, needs a second look (same vendor source).
  3. Across files. Two statements from one bank written by different programs, or a file created before its own last transaction, do not fit a normal download.

Example, not a real case. A statement opens at ₹1,20,000, shows credits of ₹3,50,000 and debits of ₹2,90,000, and closes at ₹2,10,000. The sums give ₹1,80,000. The gap of ₹30,000 is a flag to trace to the row where the running balance first stops following from the row above.

If the bank signed the PDF, an edit should show as an invalid signature, but treat that as a lead: viewers differ, and even a space added to the end of a file can change the result (BFO, 2019).

Which income red flags should you check beyond the file?

A statement can add up and still overstate income. These are checks on the money, each with a plain question for the applicant or the DSA:

  • A large credit just before the application. Where did it come from, and does a document in the file explain it?
  • Money in and out on the same day. Circular flows can lift average balance and credits without any trade behind them. Precisa says circular transactions appear in 8 to 12% of the applications it has processed; that is a vendor’s own figure, and the source gives no method.
  • Another name on the account, or an account number that differs across the documents submitted.
  • Months that do not join. A gap between two statements of one account is a missing month, not a detail.
  • Bounces, cash share and EMI load against the credits claimed as income.

Each is a question, not a verdict.

How common are edited statements?

Nobody has published an independent rate for Indian NBFCs, and we found none. Two vendors give figures from their own data. Precisa says about 12.3% of statements submitted to NBFCs contain some tampering or misrepresentation, with no method stated (Precisa, checked 2026-11-24). Inscribe, a US vendor, reports about 6 to 7% of bank statements flagged in 2025 across its network, mostly US lenders (Inscribe, checked 2026-11-24). Read both as upper bounds.

The regulator’s number is firmer but wider. Loan fraud reported by banks and financial institutions in FY26 was ₹40,774 crore across 8,640 cases, and the RBI said it included 314 older cases worth ₹30,199 crore classified afresh (Outlook Business on the RBI Annual Report 2025-26, 29 May 2026). It is not split out for NBFCs or for statements.

What review trail will an auditor accept?

An auditor wants to see who looked at which file, what they found, what they decided and who agreed, with times, and proof that the record has not been changed since. RBI’s Master Direction on Fraud Risk Management in NBFCs (15 July 2024) is the closest rule we found. It does not set a statement-review format, but three of its provisions show what the record must support (RBI/DOS/2024-25/120):

  • Evidence before a fraud tag. The NBFC must serve a show cause notice with the transactions or events relied on, allow at least 21 days to reply, and pass a reasoned order (paras 2.1.1 to 2.1.4). Your flag notes and file facts are that material.
  • Reporting clock. A Fraud Monitoring Return is due within 14 days of classification (para 6.2.1). It covers Upper and Middle Layer NBFCs, and Base Layer NBFCs with assets of ₹500 crore or more (para 1.2.1). Smaller Base Layer NBFCs are outside it; check your own position.
  • Records for auditors. Details of closed fraud cases are kept for examination by auditors (para 6.3.3).

On two people: the NBFC Credit Risk Management Directions, 2025 ask notified NBFCs (non-deposit-taking, assets ₹500 crore or more) to carry out “independent and objective credit appraisal in all cases of lending” (para 6, RBI/DOR/2025-26/350). They do not say maker-checker. A second reviewer is your own control, and your compliance team decides whether it meets your policy. Also, under the Digital Lending Directions, 2025 a lender stays “fully responsible and liable” for its service providers’ acts (para 5(vii), RBI/2025-26/36), so a DSA’s statement is still yours to review.

A trail that holds up has five parts:

  1. The file’s SHA-256 hash at upload, so you can show which file you read.
  2. Every flag closed as explained or confirmed, with a note and a name.
  3. Serious flags (a statement that does not add up, a serious tamper finding) explained away only by a credit manager.
  4. Sign-off by someone other than the person who sent the case for it.
  5. A log that cannot be quietly edited, with the report for the loan system.

What can a file check not tell you?

A statement regenerated cleanly, with correct arithmetic and an ordinary program, passes file checks. Then only the money checks and the documents around it (GST returns, ITRs, the sale deed behind a big credit) help. A genuine statement can also trip a file check, for example when a bank’s engine saves a file twice. So a flag is explained with a note, not treated as a rejection, and a person decides the loan. Scanned or photographed statements need a person too, since a text check cannot read them.

How does Cashtrail do this?

Cashtrail runs the balance, file and cross-file checks on each statement, opens one flag per finding with the rows or file facts behind it, and holds the case until every flag has a note and a second person signs off. It keeps the rows read and each file’s hash, not the PDF itself. It does not score the borrower or decide the loan.

Questions credit heads ask

Can a credit team prove a bank statement PDF was edited?

Not always. A statement that does not add up row by row, or whose file facts contradict its own dates, is strong evidence. A clean file made with a normal program proves nothing either way. Treat findings as things seen, ask the applicant for a fresh statement, and let a second person decide.

Does RBI require a maker-checker on bank statement review?

We found no RBI direction that uses the words maker or checker for statement review. The NBFC Credit Risk Management Directions, 2025 ask notified NBFCs for independent and objective credit appraisal. Whether a two-person review meets your policy is a question for your compliance team.

How long do NBFCs have to report a fraud to RBI?

Under the 2024 Master Direction, a covered NBFC files a Fraud Monitoring Return within 14 days of classifying the case as fraud. The direction covers Upper and Middle Layer NBFCs, and Base Layer NBFCs with assets of ₹500 crore or more.
