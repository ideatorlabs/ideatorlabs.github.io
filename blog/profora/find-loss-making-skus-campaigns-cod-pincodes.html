Find the SKUs, ads and COD pincodes that lose money

Where a D2C brand loses money after returns, RTO, shipping and fees, and what to do about each.

Most D2C brands lose money in three places: a few products, a few ad campaigns and a few cash-on-delivery (COD) pincode areas. You find them by splitting your settled orders, meaning delivered, returned or lost, by product, campaign and pincode, and counting every cost against each. Then give each loss one owner, one deadline, proof of the fix and a check that it paid back.

In short

  • Judge products, campaigns and pincode areas on settled orders only, after shipping, fees, returns and RTO. Store-wide averages hide all three.
  • A campaign is a loss when its ROAS is below your break-even ROAS, not when it looks low. A COD area is a loss when its RTO cost outweighs what the delivered orders earn.
  • A fix is not done when it is changed. It is done when later settled orders show it paid back.

How is this different from margin per order?

Margin per order tells you what an average order leaves you. This post is about the exceptions: the specific products, campaigns and areas that drag the average down, and who fixes them. If you have not worked out margin per order yet, start with true margin per order, then come back here.

Which products lose money after returns, RTO, shipping and fees?

Take each product and add up what it earns on delivered orders, then take away shipping, packaging, payment and COD fees, and the cost of every RTO (return to origin). Products that look fine on revenue can be negative here. Check that every product has a cost entered first, or the figure misleads.

Two checks matter. First, cost completeness: merchants on Shopify’s community forum say a profit figure “can look accurate while still being misleading if some products have no cost entered” (Shopify Community thread, July 2026; forum opinion). Second, volume: judge a product only once it has enough settled orders. Profora uses at least 20 in the window.

The fees are knowable. Razorpay lists 2% on domestic cards, UPI and netbanking plus 18% GST on the fee (Razorpay pricing). ClickPost’s Delhivery guide lists a COD charge of the higher of ₹40 or 2% of order value (ClickPost, updated 18 September 2026; confirm it against your own rate card). Shiprocket says an RTO charge applies to cover the return journey (Shiprocket support).

Which ad campaigns lose money even when ROAS looks fine?

A campaign loses money when its ROAS is below your break-even ROAS. Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by the share of order value left after product, shipping, fees and returns, before ad spend. Ads Manager does not know those costs, so its ROAS cannot tell you this.

One Shopify merchant post (August 2026) shows the effect: an ad at a 3.8 ROAS that still lost money because break-even was near 4.9, since “Meta does not know your product costs, shipping subsidies, discounts, payment fees, or refunds” (Shopify Community thread; the post uses dollars and does not mention RTO). For COD brands the courier’s result sits in another file, which is the same blind spot.

Example (made-up numbers): an order of ₹1,000 leaves ₹300 after product, shipping, fees and an allowance for returns, before ads. Break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ 0.30 = 3.33. A campaign reporting 2.6 loses money on every order. One reporting 4.0 earns.

Which COD pincode areas lose money?

An area loses money when its COD orders that come back cost more than the delivered ones earn. One store-wide RTO rate hides this. Group settled COD orders by the first three digits of the pincode, and rank areas by rupees lost, not by rate alone.

The national picture shows why COD needs its own view. ClickPost’s FY2026 network data puts COD RTO at 37.51% and prepaid at 5.28%, with a typical loaded cost of about ₹350 per RTO (range ₹285 to ₹490) at a ₹1,200 order value (ClickPost; vendor figures, not a national average). Your own areas will differ.

Example (made-up numbers): each delivered COD order earns ₹300 after product, shipping and fees, and each RTO costs ₹350. In 100 COD orders at a 37.5% RTO, that is 62.5 × 300 − 37.5 × 350, about ₹5,600 earned. In an area at 55% RTO it is 45 × 300 − 55 × 350, a loss of ₹5,750.

Be careful with thin slices. A forum reply on a COD analysis thread advises a minimum order count and ranking by absolute return cost (Shopify Community thread; forum opinion). That is why Profora judges areas at three digits, with at least 20 settled COD orders.

What should you do about each leak?

  • Product: raise the price, cut the cost, sell it prepaid only, sell it in a bundle or with a minimum order, or stop selling it. Owner: the founder. Proof: the new price, supplier quote or Shopify setting.
  • Campaign: pause it, cut the budget, change the audience or creative, or keep COD buyers out. Owner: whoever runs the ads. Proof: an Ads Manager screenshot. It spends every day, so give it a short deadline.
  • COD area: prepaid only, a part payment in advance, a different courier, or a confirmation before dispatch. Owner: operations. Proof: the checkout rule you changed.

Not every loss should be fixed. A loss leader or a launch can be kept on purpose, but write down why and who approved it. One vendor guide suggests restricting very high-RTO pincodes to prepaid or routing them to couriers with stronger delivery-attempt handling (ShipPrime, 14 July 2026; a shipping aggregator’s own content). Test options one at a time so you know which worked.

How do you know a fix paid back?

Compare the weekly result for that product, campaign or area before the fix with the settled weeks after it. Wait for at least two settled weeks, because RTO comes in late. If it is still losing after four, reopen it with the numbers.

Watch the season. Unicommerce’s April 2026 report charts RTO moving from 39.2% in November 2025 to 21.0% in March 2026 (Unicommerce; the page is unclear whether that is a market-wide or single-brand figure). A before-and-after reading can credit a fix with what the calendar did, so look at the store-wide change beside it.

How does Profora do this?

Profora is profit leak tracking for Indian D2C brands. Today you upload the exports you already download: Shopify orders, the courier report, and Meta and Google ad reports. It counts profit on settled orders and opens a leak for each loss-making product, below-break-even campaign and COD pincode area, with an owner and a deadline. You record the fix with proof. Later uploads show whether it paid back. It keeps weekly totals, not buyer names, phones or addresses. Live Shopify, courier and ad connections are not built yet.

Questions owners ask

How many orders do I need before I judge a product or a pincode area?

Enough that one bad week cannot decide it. Profora's rule is at least 20 settled orders in the window before it opens a product or pincode leak, and at least ₹10,000 of spend before it judges a campaign. Treat that as a starting point and adjust it to your volume.

Should I switch COD off in a pincode area that loses money?

Not always. Prepaid only, a small advance payment on COD, a different courier and a confirmation call before dispatch are all options. Pick one, record it, and check the next settled weeks to see whether the area stopped losing.

Is platform ROAS enough to decide which campaign to pause?

No. It does not include product cost, shipping, fees or returns. Compare each campaign with your break-even ROAS, which is 1 divided by the share of order value left before ad spend.
